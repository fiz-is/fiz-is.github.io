SEO, AI search & product-led growth / September 2026

I got 46,861 Google impressions in 15 days. That wasn't the SEO win I thought it was.

I built CodexUsage.dev around a narrow search opportunity, launched a real developer utility, and watched Google pick it up almost immediately. Then the traffic cooled, my content strategy drifted, and I had to reconsider what the numbers were actually telling me.

The easiest version of this story would be: I launched a new domain, ranked on page one for emerging AI queries within days, and earned tens of thousands of search impressions. All true. It would also leave out the most important part.

The site was already getting attention before I'd proved that attention would last, or that the people arriving were the people who needed the product. I started with a disciplined SEO hypothesis and then made decisions that blurred it. The useful lesson isn't that a new domain can rank quickly. It's that early rankings, durable search demand, and actual product growth are three different things.

The original bet was fairly specific

I was using Codex heavily and kept running into the same practical questions: how much quota was left, when it reset, and why one kind of work consumed the allowance faster than another. Official interfaces answered some of that, but the information was scattered and the behavior wasn't always intuitive.

So I built CodexUsage.dev: a free local usage companion with a macOS menu bar app, CLI, and browser dashboard, alongside documentation for limits, reset windows, and usage measurement. The utility mattered. Without it, this could easily have become one more site summarizing information already available elsewhere.

On September 2, before launch, I documented the SEO strategy. The core idea was to own a single search intent, “codex usage,” with the homepage as the best answer and a deliberately bounded supporting corpus. The exact-match domain was a hypothesis about relevance and recognition, not a substitute for useful content.

The milestones were surprisingly patient for a fast-moving category: get indexed by week four, reach the top 20 for the head term by week eight, and aim for page one by week sixteen. I planned approximately 22 carefully scoped pages, including a maintained limit-change ledger and original research that other sites could reasonably cite.

Two bets were bundled together: that I could earn visibility on a new site without a serious backlink campaign, and that the visibility would connect to a genuinely useful tool.

What actually happened

The first part happened faster than expected. The site was launched on September 3. By September 17, the head term had reached an average position of 12.5 across 302 impressions and four clicks. That beat the week-eight top-20 milestone by about six weeks. Other, newer model-related searches put pages near the top of their results almost immediately.

At first, it was exciting. Daily clicks reached 93 on September 8. The following days were a much less comfortable lesson.

The site wasn't simply collapsing, though. A later export for September 9 to 30 recorded 608 clicks and 53,441 impressions. September 23 to 29 improved over the prior week: 143 clicks versus 134, and approximately 12.6K impressions versus 11.7K. Both weeks remained below the September 9 to 15 spike. That is a more precise description than “it grew” or “it died.”

Even the head term improved in the later export: 83 clicks, 1,968 impressions, and an average position of 6.71 for “codex usage.” It was meaningful progress, but average position alone wasn't a durable outcome. I still needed to see whether the site could hold useful query coverage over time and turn it into product use.

Reading the data carefully

The September 3 to 17 and September 9 to 30 exports overlap. They are different measurement windows, not totals to add together. The later query and page exports also cannot tell me which specific query landed on which URL on which day.

What genuinely worked

Shipping something useful gave the content a reason to exist.

The site wasn't built around a generic AI-written guide. There was a functioning local tool behind it, together with dated limits information and measured experiments. The content distinguished official statements, direct observations, and reports from users. That distinction became especially important in a category where product limits were changing quickly.

A narrow, new search category created a real opening.

Speed mattered. Pages addressing fresh questions about usage limits, model availability, and reserve mechanics surfaced rapidly. On September 30, a newly published GPT-6.1 Sol page received 955 impressions and 20 clicks that same day, at an average position of 5.69. That was remarkable for a new page, but it was also the perfect example of what not to overinterpret: launch-day visibility is not a durable ranking.

Original evidence was more defensible than another explanation page.

I ran controlled model-usage studies and published methodology and limitations, including results that didn't fit intuitive assumptions about quota consumption. The change ledger and measured research gave the site assets that weren't just reformulations of official documentation. I also observed the site appearing in Google's AI Overview for the broad head query. That was encouraging evidence of citation eligibility, not proof of stable AI-search ownership or incremental traffic.

Where my execution fell short

1. I confused capturing demand with building the site I had planned.

My original topical map called for about 22 pages and strict ownership of each search intent. Within the first 15 days, the site had 32 routes. Fifteen unplanned pages had been created in the first week, before meaningful Search Console evidence was available to justify them. Six planned pages, including potential research and comparison assets, were still missing in the September 18 audit.

The new pages often ranked, which made that expansion feel validated after the fact. But the decision to create them came from monitoring product news, not from proof that they served the long-term search or acquisition strategy. It was an execution choice I could point to, not a Google algorithm I had to guess about.

2. The traffic mix told a different story from the headline number.

A large share of early demand came from new OpenAI feature terminology. In one September 18 query sample, Luna-related searches accounted for 7,870 of 12,463 impressions, or 63%, and 123 of 214 clicks. That percentage describes the sampled query set, not all site impressions.

Those pages answered real questions. But somebody looking up a just-announced model feature wasn't necessarily looking for a quota monitor. As launch interest cooled, so did much of the traffic. I had found a way to ride emerging demand before establishing that it was the audience I needed.

3. Some apparently good rankings generated surprisingly few clicks.

The September 18 page export showed the homepage at average position 12.05 with a 1.73% CTR. Meanwhile, the Luna page averaged position 6.32 with a 1.28% CTR, and the changes ledger averaged 7.32 with a 0.18% CTR.

That's not proof that any one page had a bad title or that Google was “testing” the domain in a specific way. Average position and CTR aggregate different queries, intents, layouts, and dates. It is evidence that ranking alone was a poor proxy for the commercial or user value of these pages.

4. I hadn't closed the loop from search visit to installation.

GA4 and Umami were part of the site's measurement setup, but the early retrospective did not establish a reliable search-to-install conversion series. The pages attracting the most attention were not necessarily the ones aimed at developers actively trying to solve a usage problem. I couldn't honestly claim product-led acquisition success from impressions and clicks.

5. Even my internal linking drifted away from the original strategy.

The original brief made the homepage the canonical owner of the broad query. Yet a September 18 internal-link audit found that the high-traffic Astra page linked to the homepage zero times while linking to many other pages. Several routes reused the same boilerplate “back to overview” anchor rather than contextual links. That was a concrete architectural mismatch with the strategy. Whether it measurably reduced rankings was still a hypothesis.

So what actually explains the drop?

ObservedSearch demand around newly introduced model and reserve terms was volatile. The traffic concentration, timing, and subsequent decline are visible in the September data. The site also added substantially more pages than planned, and its internal link architecture departed from the written ownership model.

Plausible, unprovenSome early exposure may have reflected temporary freshness-sensitive demand, shifting SERP competition, or Google's changing assessment of individual pages. More established sources, including official documentation and community results, appeared ahead of the site on some queries. Those mechanisms are consistent with the observations, but they weren't isolated experimentally.

Not establishedI cannot attribute the subsequent visibility changes to a Google spam update, a sitewide penalty, an exact-match-domain penalty, AI-written content, or “cannibalization” without more specific evidence. A temporal coincidence would not make any of those a causal explanation.

My biggest correction was to stop treating being shown in search as proof that I'd earned the searcher's trust, or that I'd built a sustainable acquisition channel.

That uncertainty is part of the case study, not something to edit out. An honest answer about an SEO result sometimes has to be narrower than the most satisfying explanation.

What I learned

  1. Set the success criterion before the spike. The original week-eight and week-sixteen ranking milestones were useful precisely because they kept me from declaring an early result a final outcome. A two-week site's launch graph is not a mature growth curve.
  2. Separate three scorecards. Search discovery measures whether a page gets shown. Search usefulness includes query fit, click behavior, and repeatable visibility. Product acquisition requires actual use or conversion. A project can move one scorecard without moving the others.
  3. Don't let emerging queries silently rewrite the topical strategy. A new term can deserve coverage, but it should pass a test of relevance, distinct intent, information gain, and a clear job in the product journey. More URLs are not the same as more authority.
  4. Evidence has to stay attached to its scope. A measured quota movement in one controlled run isn't a universal limit formula. An AI Overview screenshot isn't stable citation share. A page-level CTR and query-level position aren't a causal explanation. The temptation to round those distinctions away is especially strong when the early numbers look good.

What I would do differently on a real growth team

First, I'd make the original search hypothesis and URL ownership map a working constraint, not a document I could quietly outgrow. New pages would need to identify the specific search job they serve and why an existing page cannot satisfy it.

Second, I'd segment acquisition reporting from day one: head-term and evergreen usage queries, product-event queries, and high-intent troubleshooting queries would each have their own visibility, clicks, and eventual conversion picture. I would avoid rolling all impressions into one headline that obscures the audience mix.

Third, I'd instrument the actual conversion path: organic landing page to installer click to successful installation or local activation, where this can be measured without compromising the product's privacy posture. That would tell me whether a page about hitting a usage limit is genuinely more valuable than a high-volume feature explainer.

Finally, I'd allocate more attention to the content competitors cannot reproduce quickly: first-party measurements, maintained change histories, clear source provenance, and useful product workflows. AI assisted with research and implementation, but I remained responsible for deciding what was publishable, challenging incorrect claims, and separating observation from interpretation.

The outcome isn't a clean SEO victory or a failed launch. I shipped a useful product, demonstrated rapid search discovery, got meaningful head-term traction ahead of the original plan, and uncovered weaknesses in my own strategy and measurement. The honest portfolio story is the quality of those decisions and corrections, not an inflated claim of durable growth.

Evidence, time windows, and limitations

Source material. The original topical map is dated September 2, 2026. The September 18 strategic audit compared that plan with the repository and a September 3 to 17 Google Search Console export. A later Search Console analysis covered September 9 to 30. This draft was prepared from those dated first-party records and repository documentation, not from a current Search Console pull.

Comparability. Search Console's query and page exports describe different aggregations. They do not expose a day-by-day query-to-landing-page join. Overlapping windows are not additive. Average positions and CTRs are descriptive aggregates, not controlled experiments.

What's still missing. A complete post-September trend, verified install attribution, and linked raw charts/screenshots would strengthen this article before publication. The September core update, AI Overview behavior, and the effects of internal-link changes have not been causally established here.
